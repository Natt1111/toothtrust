## Summary

Apical periodontitis (AP) is an inflammatory condition of the periradicular tissues resulting from microbial infection of the root canal system. It encompasses a spectrum of conditions from asymptomatic chronic inflammation to acute symptomatic abscess. The AAE's diagnostic classification system (published in the *Journal of Endodontics*, 2009) provides standardized terminology for pulpal and periapical diagnoses that correlates clinical findings with histopathological states and informs treatment selection.

Accurate diagnosis requires integration of subjective symptoms, objective tests (thermal, electric pulp testing, palpation, percussion), and radiographic assessment. The AAE classification distinguishes between pulpal diagnoses and periapical diagnoses, which must be documented separately.

---

## Clinical Indications

### AAE Periapical Diagnostic Categories

**1. Normal Apical Tissues**
- No symptoms; negative percussion/palpation; radiographically normal PDL space.
- Management: None (if associated with a vital pulp) or routine monitoring.

**2. Symptomatic Apical Periodontitis (SAP)**
- Pain on biting, percussion sensitivity, possible palpation tenderness.
- Radiograph: normal PDL widening to frank radiolucency (AP may be early).
- Associated pulp status: may be vital, irreversibly inflamed, or necrotic.
- Treatment: root canal treatment or tooth removal.

**3. Asymptomatic Apical Periodontitis (AAP)**
- No pain; identified incidentally on radiograph.
- Radiolucency at apex (periapical lesion/granuloma).
- Pulp is typically non-vital (necrotic) based on negative pulp tests.
- Treatment: root canal treatment to eliminate the source of infection.

**4. Acute Apical Abscess**
- Rapid onset of pain, swelling; systemic signs possible (fever, lymphadenopathy).
- May or may not have a radiographic lesion (early abscess may not yet show).
- Pus formation through cortical bone; fluctuant swelling or draining sinus.
- Treatment: emergency drainage (incision/drainage or access opening), antibiotics if systemic involvement, definitive RCT or extraction.

**5. Chronic Apical Abscess**
- Sinus tract (parulis) present — draining fistula through mucosa or skin.
- Mild or no pain due to established drainage pathway.
- Radiograph: typically periapical radiolucency.
- Treatment: definitive RCT or extraction after sinus tract tracing.

**6. Condensing Osteitis**
- Diffuse radiopaque lesion around root apex; reaction to low-grade infection.
- Often asymptomatic.
- Pulp may be vital with irreversible pulpitis or necrotic.
- Treatment: address pulpal source; condensing osteitis typically resolves after RCT.

### Pulpal Diagnosis (Required Alongside Periapical Diagnosis)
- Normal pulp / Reversible pulpitis / Irreversible pulpitis (symptomatic or asymptomatic) / Pulp necrosis / Previously treated / Previously initiated therapy.

## Contraindications

- **Antibiotic treatment alone**: Antibiotics do not adequately treat apical periodontitis. Emergency surgical drainage and/or RCT are required; antibiotics are adjunctive for systemic spread.
- **Vital pulp therapy in teeth with necrotic pulp**: IPC/DPC are contraindicated when pulp tests indicate necrosis; the infection source must be eliminated via RCT.
- **Watchful waiting for symptomatic lesions**: Active apical periodontitis (SAP, acute abscess) requires treatment; delaying increases risk of space infection spread.

## Evidence Notes

- **Prevalence**: AP has a high prevalence in adults — a radiographic survey of European adults showed 52% of individuals had at least one tooth with AP (Frisk & Hakeberg, 2006).
- **Histopathology**: The majority (>95%) of periapical radiolucencies are periapical granulomas or cysts, not neoplastic lesions; biopsy is indicated for atypical lesions (no response to RCT, unusual imaging features).
- **Healing after RCT**: Grung et al. (1990) and subsequent reviews show that 70–85% of asymptomatic AP lesions show complete radiographic resolution within 2–4 years post-RCT.
- **CBCT superiority**: CBCT detects AP earlier than conventional radiography; Lofthag-Hansen et al. (2007) found 62% more periapical lesions with CBCT vs. periapical films in the same patients.
- **Microbiology**: AP is a polymicrobial anaerobic infection; Enterococcus faecalis is disproportionately prevalent in failed RCT cases (about 30–40%) compared to primary infections, driving retreatment challenges.
