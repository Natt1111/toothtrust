## Summary

Cracked tooth syndrome (CTS) encompasses a spectrum of incomplete tooth fractures that can range from superficial enamel crazes to complete crown-root fractures extending below the alveolar crest. The AAE classifies tooth fractures into five categories based on location and severity. Diagnosis is challenging — cracks are often radiographically occult, symptoms are intermittent and poorly localized, and the condition is frequently misdiagnosed or diagnosed late. Early identification and treatment can prevent pulpal involvement and tooth loss.

The key clinical features are sharp pain on biting (particularly on release), cold sensitivity, and difficulty localizing the offending tooth. Treatment depends entirely on the extent and location of the fracture.

---

## Clinical Indications

### AAE Fracture Classification and Management

**1. Craze Lines**
- Superficial enamel fractures; no symptoms; common in all adult teeth.
- No treatment required; monitoring only.

**2. Fractured Cusp**
- Cusp separates at or near the gingival margin; typically involves dentin; pulp usually not involved.
- Pain on biting may be present; cold sensitive.
- Treatment: composite restoration, onlay, or crown depending on extent; vital pulp therapy if pulp exposed.

**3. Cracked Tooth (Incomplete Fracture)**
- Fracture extends from occlusal surface toward, but not through, the root; remains an incomplete fracture.
- Symptoms: sharp pain on biting (especially release), thermal sensitivity (especially cold lingering), difficult localization.
- Diagnosis tools: transillumination, methylene blue dye, bite stick (Tooth Slooth), selective anesthesia, CBCT.
- Treatment: Crown is primary treatment to prevent propagation; if pulp is involved → RCT + crown; if symptoms do not resolve after crown → further evaluation or extraction.

**4. Split Tooth**
- Complete fracture dividing tooth into two segments; typically the endpoint of an untreated cracked tooth.
- Tooth typically non-restorable as a whole; potential for hemisection or root resection if anatomy allows, otherwise extraction.

**5. Vertical Root Fracture (VRF)**
- Fracture extends longitudinally within root; most common in endodontically treated teeth (post or lateral compaction forces).
- Symptoms: recurrent abscess, J-shaped radiolucency, sinus tract.
- No treatment can save a tooth with VRF (except hemisection in multi-rooted tooth if only one root affected); extraction required.

### Diagnostic Protocol for Cracked Tooth
1. Complete history — onset, character, triggers (cold, biting, release pain).
2. Percussion/bite testing — Tooth Slooth or cotton roll; isolate cusps to localize.
3. Transillumination — interrupted transillumination pattern is pathognomonic for crack.
4. Methylene blue dye — stains crack lines for visualization.
5. Periodontal probing — narrow isolated pocket may indicate vertical crack propagation into root.
6. Selective anesthesia — PDL block to localize pain to a single tooth.
7. CBCT — detect sub-gingival fracture extent; aids in prognosis.
8. Pulp vitality testing — determine pulp status.

## Contraindications

- **Crown placement without RCT when irreversible pulpitis is present**: If cold test shows lingering >30 seconds, RCT must precede crown placement.
- **Delay in treatment**: Incomplete fractures are time-sensitive; delayed diagnosis allows crack propagation below the crestal bone, making the tooth unsalvageable.
- **VRF tooth retention with RCT/retreatment**: Root canal treatment cannot bridge a vertical root fracture; attempting RCT wastes resources and delays appropriate management.

## Evidence Notes

- **Diagnostic accuracy**: Cameron (1976) first described CTS; modern studies report sensitivity of transillumination ~56–70% and methylene blue ~74–80% for incomplete fractures.
- **Crown success for cracked teeth**: A retrospective study (Opdam et al., 2008) found that ~80% of cracked teeth treated with a crown were still present at 5 years; teeth that required RCT in addition to crown had higher failure rates.
- **RCT after crown for cracks**: About 20% of cracked teeth treated with crown eventually require RCT within 5 years (Krell & Rivera, 2007); patients should be counseled pre-treatment.
- **VRF in endodontically treated teeth**: Prevalence of VRF is 11–20% in extracted endodontically treated teeth (Tsesis et al., 2010); lateral condensation technique and large posts are risk factors.
- **MTA/mineral trioxide aggregate**: In cases of incomplete fracture with pulp involvement, MTA pulp capping before crown placement may extend pulp vitality and defer RCT.
- **CBCT limitations**: CBCT can miss hairline incomplete fractures; a negative CBCT does not exclude cracked tooth.
