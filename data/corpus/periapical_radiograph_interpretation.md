## Summary

Periapical (PA) radiographs provide visualization of the entire tooth — crown, root(s), periapical region, and surrounding alveolar bone — and are the primary imaging modality for endodontic diagnosis, periapical pathology evaluation, and assessment of root and bone morphology. The full-mouth series (FMX) includes 14–18 periapical images combined with bitewing radiographs for a comprehensive survey.

The paralleling technique is the standard for periapical radiography; it minimizes dimensional distortion by positioning the receptor parallel to the tooth's long axis with the beam perpendicular to both. The bisecting angle technique is an alternative when patient anatomy or tori prevent paralleling but introduces more distortion.

## Clinical Indications

### Systematic Periapical Interpretation Sequence

**Step 1 — Technical Quality**
- Adequate density and contrast; target organ (apex and periapex) clearly visible.
- Entire tooth and 3–5 mm beyond the apex included in the image.
- Minimal geometric distortion; paralleling technique preferred.
- No film artifacts (pressure marks, chemical stains on film; noise/mottling on digital).

**Step 2 — Crown Evaluation**
- Caries: proximal, occlusal, cervical.
- Existing restorations: material, margin integrity, extent relative to pulp.
- Crown fractures: vertical or horizontal lines, rarely visible on PA.

**Step 3 — Pulp Chamber and Canals**
- Chamber size relative to age expectation.
- Canal calcification (diffuse or focal; pulp stones).
- Internal resorption: symmetric balloon-shaped canal widening.
- Existing root canal treatment: material density, length, fill quality (short, overfill, void).

**Step 4 — Root Morphology**
- Number of roots and canals: bifurcation or trifurcation level.
- Root curvature: degree and direction (S-curve, dilaceration).
- Root length and width.
- External root resorption: shortening, surface irregularity.
- Hypercementosis: bulbous root end (non-pathologic but important for extraction planning).

**Step 5 — Periodontal Ligament Space**
- Normal: thin radiolucent line, uniform (~0.15–0.38 mm).
- Widened PDL: indicates increased function (bruxism, abutment stress), early periapical inflammation, or early cemental resorption.
- Loss of lamina dura: associated with periapical pathology or systemic disease (hyperparathyroidism, Paget's).

**Step 6 — Periapical Region**
- Normal: well-corticated lamina dura; dense trabecular bone.
- Periapical granuloma: well-defined radiolucency at or near apex; most common periapical pathology.
- Periapical cyst: indistinguishable from granuloma on radiograph alone; requires biopsy.
- Condensing osteitis: diffuse radiopacity at apex; reaction to low-grade pulp inflammation.
- Periapical abscess: may appear as ill-defined radiolucency; clinical signs (swelling, sinus) support diagnosis.
- Hypercementosis: bulbous apex; non-pathologic.
- Normal anatomic variants: nasopalatine canal, mental foramen, maxillary sinus superimposition — may mimic pathology.

**Step 7 — Alveolar Bone**
- Crestal bone height and morphology.
- Furcation status in multi-rooted teeth.
- Vertical bony defects (angular bone loss).

**Step 8 — Adjacent Structures**
- Maxillary sinus: floor cortication, mucosal thickening, opacification.
- Inferior alveolar canal proximity (important for mandibular extractions/implants).
- Mental foramen position.

## Contraindications

- **PA radiograph as substitute for CBCT when 3D information is needed**: For assessment of buccal/lingual bone, implant planning, or VRF evaluation, CBCT is required.
- **Using outdated PAs without updated series**: Periapical pathology can change rapidly (weeks to months); treatment decisions should use current radiographs.
- **Excessive radiation from repeated PAs without clinical justification**: Each diagnostic exposure should add clinical value.

## Evidence Notes

- **Sensitivity for periapical lesions**: Periapical radiographs have ~60–85% sensitivity for detecting established periapical lesions; buccal or lingual cortical plate lesions are often invisible until they erode through cortical bone. CBCT increases detection by ~15–50%.
- **Diagnosis accuracy**: Ridler et al. (1992) and Lofthag-Hansen et al. (2007) found that CBCT detected significantly more periapical lesions than conventional radiography in endodontically treated teeth.
- **Anatomic superimposition**: Maxillary posterior teeth frequently have sinus floor and zygomatic buttress superimposed on roots; mental foramen is commonly misread as periapical pathology in mandibular premolars.
- **Digital advantage**: Image enhancement (brightness, contrast, sharpness filters) in digital periapical radiographs improves subtle lesion detection; studies show ~10–20% improvement in sensitivity over unenhanced digital or film.
- **Endodontic working length**: The electronic apex locator (EAL) has largely supplemented radiographic working length estimation; EAL reduces radiation exposure and improves accuracy to ±0.5 mm.
