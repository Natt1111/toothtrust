## Summary

Accurately differentiating gingivitis from periodontitis is foundational to appropriate treatment planning, correct CDT coding, and patient counseling. The key distinction lies in whether irreversible clinical attachment loss (CAL) has occurred. Gingivitis is a reversible inflammatory condition of the gingiva with no attachment loss; periodontitis involves irreversible loss of supporting periodontal structures (bone, PDL, cementum). The 2017 AAP classification formalizes these distinctions.

Many patients have features of both — gingivitis superimposed on a periodontitis-reduced periodontium — which adds diagnostic nuance that must be correctly documented.

---

## Clinical Indications

### Gingivitis — Defining Features

**Plaque-Induced Gingivitis on Intact Periodontium**
- Gingival inflammation (BOP, redness, edema, altered contour).
- Probing depths may be elevated due to gingival enlargement (pseudo-pockets) but CAL = 0.
- No radiographic bone loss.
- **Reversible with professional debridement and improved home care.**
- Treatment: prophylaxis (D1110); oral hygiene instruction.

**Gingivitis on Reduced Periodontium (Post-Periodontitis)**
- Gingival inflammation in a patient who has been successfully treated for periodontitis.
- Reduced but stable CAL and bone levels.
- Active disease is controlled; inflammation is biofilm-induced at existing pockets.
- Treatment: periodontal maintenance (D4910), NOT re-treatment with SRP.

**Non-Plaque-Induced Gingival Conditions**
- Drug-influenced gingival enlargement (cyclosporine, calcium channel blockers, phenytoin).
- Hormonal gingivitis (pregnancy, puberty).
- Herpetic gingivostomatitis, desquamative gingivitis (mucous membrane pemphigoid, lichen planus).
- Requires identification and management of systemic/drug cause; CDT D9999 or narrative codes.

### Periodontitis — Defining Features

**Clinical Signs of Periodontitis:**
- **Clinical attachment loss (CAL) ≥1 mm**: The non-negotiable diagnostic criterion.
- Radiographic alveolar bone loss: Horizontal or vertical; detectable at ≥30–40% mineral loss.
- Probing depths ≥4 mm with BOP, or pockets in presence of recession.
- Furcation involvement in multi-rooted teeth.
- Tooth mobility (advanced cases).

**Pitfalls in Diagnosis:**
- **Recession without probing depth increase**: CAL is present even when PD is 3 mm but recession is 3 mm (total CAL = 6 mm).
- **Hyperplasia masking attachment loss**: Pseudo-pockets from gingival overgrowth may inflate PD without true CAL; calculate CAL from CEJ.
- **Radiographic limitations**: Up to 40% bone loss can occur before radiographic detection on conventional 2D films.

### Differential Diagnosis Flowchart

```
BOP + gingival inflammation present?
    ↓ Yes
CAL present? (confirmed by probing and radiograph)
    ↓ No → GINGIVITIS (reversible; prophylaxis/OHI)
    ↓ Yes
Bone loss present?
    ↓ No → Possible localized attachment loss; re-measure
    ↓ Yes → PERIODONTITIS (Stage/Grade per 2017 AAP; SRP indicated)
```

## Contraindications

- **Treating periodontitis as gingivitis**: SRP not performed; patient receives only prophylaxis (D1110) while disease progresses. This is a common medicolegal error.
- **Coding D4910 (maintenance) for an untreated patient**: D4910 is specifically for previously treated periodontitis; a new patient with active disease requires D4341/D4342 (SRP), not D4910.
- **Using BOP alone as a periodontitis indicator**: BOP without CAL may indicate only gingivitis; treatment decisions must be based on CAL and bone loss.

## Evidence Notes

- **CAL as gold standard**: Clinical attachment level remains the primary diagnostic criterion for distinguishing gingivitis from periodontitis; this is universally accepted in the periodontal literature and the 2017 AAP classification.
- **Radiographic sensitivity**: Van der Velden (1993) and subsequent work show that conventional radiographs underdetect early bone loss; ~2 mm of bone must be lost before reliable radiographic detection.
- **Gingivitis reversibility**: A classic experimental gingivitis study by Löe et al. (1965) confirmed that plaque-induced gingivitis is completely reversible with proper oral hygiene; periodontitis is not.
- **Gingivitis as risk factor**: Active gingivitis is a risk factor for periodontitis onset; Schätzle et al. (2003, 26-year longitudinal study) found a 70% increase in periodontitis risk with persistent gingivitis.
- **Pregnancy gingivitis**: The prevalence of pregnancy gingivitis approaches 30–100% in some studies; it resolves post-partum with proper care and does not necessarily progress to periodontitis.
