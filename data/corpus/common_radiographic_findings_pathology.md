## Summary

Radiographic interpretation in dentistry requires recognition of both normal anatomic variants and pathologic findings. Misidentification of normal structures as pathology (false positives) leads to unnecessary treatment; failure to recognize genuine pathology (false negatives) causes diagnostic delay and patient harm. This reference guide covers the most common radiographic findings encountered in general dental practice, including their appearance, significance, and clinical correlates.

Each finding is characterized by radiographic density (radiolucent, radiopaque, or mixed), location, borders, and associated clinical features.

## Clinical Indications

### Radiolucent Lesions (Periapical Region)

**Periapical Granuloma**
- Most common periapical pathology (~65–75% of periapical radiolucencies).
- Round/oval radiolucency at or near apex; ≤2 cm; well-defined but not corticated border.
- Associated with non-vital tooth; negative pulp test.
- Treatment: RCT; resolves in most cases with healing.

**Periapical Cyst (Radicular Cyst)**
- ~15–25% of periapical radiolucencies; clinically/radiographically indistinguishable from granuloma.
- May be slightly larger; may have thin corticated border.
- Biopsy differentiates; may require surgical enucleation for large lesions or after RCT failure.

**Periapical Abscess**
- Often presents with ill-defined margins (spreading inflammation).
- Associated acute clinical findings: swelling, tenderness, sinus tract.
- Condensing osteitis may occur in the same tooth over time.

**Condensing Osteitis**
- Diffuse radiopacity (not lucency) at apex of tooth with chronic low-grade pulp irritation.
- Often associated with lower posterior teeth with deep caries or large restorations.
- After RCT: typically resolves over months to years.

### Radiolucent Lesions (Jaws — Beyond Periapex)

**Dentigerous Cyst**
- Well-corticated unilocular radiolucency surrounding the crown of an unerupted/impacted tooth.
- Most common around impacted mandibular third molars and maxillary canines.
- Requires extraction of associated tooth and cyst enucleation; biopsy mandatory.

**Ameloblastoma**
- Multilocular or unilocular "soap bubble" radiolucency; often associated with impacted third molar.
- Locally aggressive; requires surgical resection; biopsy essential before treatment.
- Not to be confused with dentigerous cyst on radiograph — distinguishing features include expansion and boundary behavior.

**Odontogenic Keratocyst (OKC)**
- Well-defined unilocular or multilocular radiolucency; may be large with scalloped borders.
- High recurrence rate; part of Gorlin syndrome (basal cell nevus syndrome).
- Treatment: enucleation with Carnoy's solution; careful follow-up.

**Nasopalatine Canal Cyst**
- Oval, heart-shaped, or round radiolucency between maxillary central incisors.
- Often misread as periapical pathology of #8 or #9; pulp vitality tests are positive.
- Treatment: enucleation if enlarging or symptomatic; asymptomatic small cysts may be monitored.

### Radiopaque Lesions

**Torus Palatinus / Torus Mandibularis**
- Dense, well-defined radiopacity on midline palate (torus palatinus) or lingual mandible (torus mandibularis).
- Normal anatomic variant; no treatment unless prosthetic interference.

**Calcified Lymph Node / Phlebolith**
- Irregular radiopacity, often circular, in soft tissue region adjacent to jaws.
- May be confused with salivary stone or impacted tooth fragment.
- Correlate with clinical findings; often asymptomatic.

**Exostoses / Osteoma**
- Localized bony projection; may simulate calcification on radiograph.
- No treatment needed unless functional or prosthetic concern.

**Hypercementosis**
- Bulbous enlargement of root apex due to excess cementum deposition.
- Non-pathologic; associated with Paget's disease, trauma, functional occlusal stress.
- Clinical importance: complicates extraction; root is retained in socket.

**Enamel Pearl**
- Small spherical radiopacity on root surface, typically near furcation.
- Normal variant; may contribute to periodontal defects at furcation.

### Mixed Density Lesions

**Fibro-Osseous Lesions (Cemento-Ossifying Fibroma, Ossifying Fibroma)**
- Mixed radiolucent-radiopaque lesion; well-defined border; unilocular.
- Benign; requires biopsy for definitive classification; surgical removal if symptomatic or enlarging.

**Complex/Compound Odontoma**
- Compound odontoma: tooth-like structures (denticles); multiple small radiopaque units surrounded by radiolucent halo.
- Complex odontoma: disorganized calcified mass; radiopaque center, radiolucent periphery.
- Benign; requires removal if blocking eruption.

## Contraindications

- **Treating a periapical radiolucency without pulp vitality testing**: Anatomic variants (nasopalatine canal, mental foramen) and non-odontogenic pathology can mimic periapical granuloma; vitality tests are essential.
- **Monitoring an enlarging lesion without biopsy**: Progressive radiolucent lesions must be biopsied; watchful waiting for >3–6 months without a diagnosis is not appropriate.

---

## Evidence Notes

- **Periapical lesion accuracy**: Radiographic differentiation between granuloma and cyst is unreliable (White & Goaz, classic radiology text); histopathological examination is required for definitive diagnosis.
- **Incidental findings**: Large-FOV CBCT studies find incidental findings in 45–90% of scans (Ritter et al., 2014); systematic interpretation protocols are essential.
- **Malignancy**: Malignant jaw lesions (metastases, primary jaw tumors) are rare but may present as aggressive radiolucent lesions with ill-defined, irregular borders and root resorption; clinical suspicion should prompt biopsy.
