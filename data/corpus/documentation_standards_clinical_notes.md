## Summary

Proper clinical documentation is one of the most important responsibilities in dental practice. It serves four critical functions: (1) continuity of care and treatment planning; (2) medicolegal protection; (3) insurance claim support and audit defense; and (4) communication between providers. Dental records are legal documents; incomplete, illegible, or altered notes are among the leading causes of adverse malpractice outcomes and insurance fraud investigations.

Documentation standards are governed by state dental practice acts, HIPAA Privacy Rule requirements, ADA guidelines, and individual payer contracts. Every entry should be timely, accurate, objective, complete, and legible.

## Clinical Indications

### Minimum Documentation Requirements for Common Encounters

**New Patient Examination**
- Chief complaint and complete health history (medical, dental, medications, allergies).
- Clinical examination findings: soft tissue, periodontal, dental, occlusal, TMJ.
- Periodontal chart (6-point probing).
- Radiographic interpretation with findings.
- Diagnosis and treatment plan with CDT codes and estimated fees.
- Informed consent: treatment options, risks/benefits, alternatives discussed; patient signature.
- HIPAA Notice of Privacy Practices acknowledgment.

**Restorative Visit**
- Tooth number(s), surfaces, and diagnosis justifying treatment.
- Anesthesia type, agent, and dose (e.g., "2 carpules 2% lidocaine 1:100,000 epi; buccal infiltration and IAN block, #30").
- Radiographic and clinical findings supporting treatment (caries depth, previous restoration breakdown).
- Materials used (composite shade, brand if relevant).
- Pulp status at conclusion of visit (vital, capped, near exposure).
- Post-op instructions given.
- Patient's subjective response.

**Endodontic Treatment**
- Pulpal and periapical diagnosis (AAE terminology).
- Anesthesia record.
- Rubber dam use documented.
- Working length determination method and measurements (electronic apex locator, radiograph).
- Files/instruments used, sizes, and sequence.
- Irrigation protocol (NaOCl concentration, frequency, final rinse).
- Obturation technique and material; obturation length radiograph obtained.
- Complications noted (separated instrument, perforation, bleeding).
- Post-op radiograph description.

**Periodontal SRP**
- Current periodontal chart referenced.
- Quadrant(s) treated; local anesthesia details.
- Instruments used (ultrasonics, hand instruments).
- Clinical findings at treatment sites (pocket depths, calculus burden).
- Adjuncts placed (localized antibiotic, CHX chip).
- Post-op instructions and re-evaluation appointment scheduled.

**Crown Preparation**
- Indication for crown (tooth structure loss, fracture, post-endo, etc.).
- Anesthesia details.
- Preparation design (margin type, location relative to gingiva).
- Temporary restoration type, luting agent.
- Shade selection method and shade chosen.
- Impressions/scans taken (material or digital system).
- Next appointment scheduled.

### SOAP Note Format
A structured SOAP note ensures completeness:
- **S (Subjective)**: What the patient reports — symptoms, concerns, changes since last visit.
- **O (Objective)**: Examination findings — measurements, test results, radiographic observations.
- **A (Assessment)**: Diagnosis using recognized terminology.
- **P (Plan)**: Treatment rendered today + future plan; referrals made; patient education provided.

### HIPAA Compliance
- Patient records must be maintained for a minimum period per state law (typically 7–10 years; 21 years for pediatric patients from birthday in many states).
- Electronic records must have access controls, audit trails, and encrypted backup.
- Patients have the right to access and receive a copy of their records.

## Contraindications

- **Altering records**: Post-hoc alterations to records without documentation of the change (date, author, reason) are illegal and constitute the most damaging evidence in malpractice litigation.
- **Blanket "no abnormalities" entries**: Generic, non-specific entries do not satisfy the documentation standard of care; specific findings must be recorded.
- **Delegating documentation without supervision**: Dental assistants may document objective findings under some practice acts; diagnoses and assessments must be entered by the licensed provider.

## Evidence Notes

- **Malpractice correlation**: A Dentist Insurance Company (TDIC) analysis of closed malpractice claims found that documentation deficiencies (incomplete records, missing informed consent, absent radiographs) were contributing factors in >60% of cases where the dentist was unable to defend the claim.
- **Electronic vs. paper**: EHR systems with mandatory field completion improve documentation completeness; however, auto-populated or template-only entries that don't reflect the actual clinical encounter are a growing audit risk.
- **Informed consent documentation**: Documented informed consent discussion — not just a signed form — is the legal standard; the note should record what risks were discussed, alternatives offered, and that the patient's questions were answered.
- **Amendment protocol**: When correcting a record error, the correct method is to draw a single line through the error (paper), add the correction with the date, time, and provider signature, and document the reason for correction. Electronic records must preserve an audit trail.
