## Summary

Standardized terminology is essential for consistent clinical communication, accurate insurance documentation, and defensible records. The AAE Glossary of Endodontic Terms (9th Edition) is the authoritative reference for endodontic terminology in the United States and is adopted by major dental publications and insurance coding guidelines. This document covers the most clinically relevant terms from the AAE glossary, organized by category, with emphasis on the terms that matter most for diagnosis, treatment planning, and documentation.

---

## Clinical Indications

### Pulpal Diagnoses (AAE 2009 Consensus Classification)

**Normal Pulp**
A clinical diagnostic category indicating that the pulp is symptom-free and responds normally to pulp testing. No treatment required.

**Reversible Pulpitis**
Inflammation of the pulp that can resolve once the irritant is removed. Characterized by a sharp, brief response to cold stimulus that resolves within seconds of stimulus removal. No spontaneous pain. Indication: caries removal and restoration.

**Irreversible Pulpitis — Symptomatic**
Pulpal inflammation that will not resolve. Clinical signs: prolonged, lingering response to cold (>30 seconds after stimulus removal); spontaneous or unprovoked pain; intense response to heat. Indication: root canal treatment.

**Irreversible Pulpitis — Asymptomatic**
Irreversibly inflamed pulp without clinical symptoms. Pulp is vital but not capable of healing. Often identified by radiographic evidence of large caries or incidental finding. Indication: root canal treatment.

**Pulp Necrosis**
Death of the pulp tissue. Pulp does not respond to cold, EPT, or heat testing. Radiograph may show periapical pathology. Often associated with prior trauma, long-standing untreated caries. Indication: root canal treatment.

**Previously Treated**
A tooth that has previously received root canal treatment. May have gutta-percha or other fill material. Indication: retreatment if failing; no treatment if asymptomatic and radiographically healing.

**Previously Initiated Therapy**
A tooth where pulp has been partially treated (pulpotomy, emergency access opening with or without medicament placement). Indication: completion of root canal treatment.

### Periapical Diagnoses (AAE 2009 Consensus)

**Normal Apical Tissues** — No symptoms; no radiographic pathology. No treatment for periapical condition.

**Symptomatic Apical Periodontitis (SAP)** — Pain on biting, percussion sensitivity, possible swelling. Requires treatment.

**Asymptomatic Apical Periodontitis (AAP)** — Periapical lesion (radiolucency) without symptoms; necrotic pulp typically. Requires treatment.

**Acute Apical Abscess** — Rapid onset, swelling, pus, systemic signs possible. Emergency treatment required.

**Chronic Apical Abscess** — Draining sinus tract (parulis); minimal pain. Definitive treatment required.

**Condensing Osteitis** — Diffuse radiopacity at apex; low-grade pulp inflammatory response. Treat underlying pulp condition.

### Anatomic and Procedural Terms

**Apex**: The tip of the root; the most apical point of the root canal system.

**Working Length**: The established length from a coronal reference point to the point where instruments/obturation material should terminate (typically 0.5–1 mm short of the radiographic apex, at the apical constriction).

**Apical Constriction**: The narrowest diameter of the root canal, located approximately 0.5–2 mm from the radiographic apex; the ideal apical limit of canal preparation.

**Apical Foramen**: The main apical opening of the root canal at the apical surface; may not coincide with the apex.

**Canal Isthmuses/Fins**: Connecting passageways between canals; common in mesial roots of mandibular molars and palatal roots; difficult to debride with rotary files.

**Glide Path**: A smooth, reproducible path from canal orifice to apical foramen; established before rotary instrumentation to prevent instrument fracture.

**Obturation**: Filling/sealing of the cleaned and shaped root canal system; ideally three-dimensional to prevent microleakage.

**Vertical Condensation**: Hot gutta-percha obturation technique using heat and vertical pressure to achieve 3D fill; Schilder technique.

**Lateral Condensation**: Cold gutta-percha obturation technique using spreaders to compact gutta-percha laterally; associated with higher VRF risk compared to warm techniques.

**Mineral Trioxide Aggregate (MTA)**: Calcium silicate-based material used for pulp capping, pulpotomy, apexification, perforation repair, and root-end fills; gold standard for many vital pulp therapy procedures.

**Biodentine**: Calcium silicate-based material with handling advantages over MTA; used for similar indications.

**Periradicular Surgery (Apicoectomy)**: Surgical removal of the root apex and periapical pathology, followed by retrograde root-end preparation and fill (typically with MTA).

**Vertical Root Fracture (VRF)**: A longitudinal fracture of the root that originates in the root canal and extends to the periodontium; non-treatable; requires extraction or hemisection.

**Ledge**: An iatrogenic irregularity in the canal wall caused by instrument deviation; prevents full apical instrumentation.

**Perforation**: An iatrogenic communication between the root canal system and the periodontium; requires immediate repair with MTA or bioceramic materials.

**Regenerative Endodontic Procedure (REP)**: A biologically-based procedure that replaces damaged pulp-dentin complex in immature permanent teeth with necrotic pulps; aims to promote continued root development.

**Apexification**: Induction of calcified barrier formation at the apex of an immature tooth with a necrotic pulp; MTA plug or Ca(OH)₂ barrier technique.

## Contraindications

- **Using non-standard terminology in records**: Insurance claims and medicolegal records require standardized AAE terminology; non-specific terms ("bad pulp," "infected tooth") are not adequate diagnoses.
- **Confusing AAP periapical diagnosis with AAP (American Academy of Periodontology)**: Both organizations abbreviate as "AAP"; context distinguishes usage.

## Evidence Notes

- **AAE Glossary editions**: The glossary is periodically updated; the 9th Edition reflects current diagnostic consensus. Available at aae.org/specialty/clinical-resources/glossary-endodontic-terms.
- **Diagnostic terminology in insurance**: CDT code selection for endodontic procedures maps to the pulpal diagnosis; using correct AAE terminology strengthens claims for medical necessity.
- **Pulp testing accuracy**: Cold testing (ethyl chloride, ice, Endo-Ice) has sensitivity of ~83% and specificity of ~93% for pulp vitality (Petersson et al., 1999); electric pulp testing (EPT) has similar accuracy. Both test neuronal function, not pulp vascularity.
