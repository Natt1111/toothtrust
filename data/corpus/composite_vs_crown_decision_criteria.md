## Summary

One of the most consequential and frequently contested decisions in restorative dentistry is whether a tooth warrants a direct composite resin restoration or a full-coverage crown. The choice has significant implications for tooth structure preservation, cost, longevity, and patient experience. Minimally invasive dentistry (MID) principles favor the most conservative intervention that provides adequate function and durability. However, certain clinical scenarios genuinely require the additional protection and retention that a crown provides.

This document synthesizes evidence-based criteria to guide the composite-vs-crown decision, drawing on biomechanical research, longitudinal survival studies, and clinical guidelines from the ADA and dental schools' consensus panels.

---

## Clinical Indications

### Favor Composite (Direct Restoration)

- **Small-to-moderate caries**: Class I and Class II lesions where preparation can maintain cuspal integrity and the remaining tooth structure provides adequate support.
- **Isthmus width less than one-third of intercuspal distance**: Biomechanical studies support composite as the appropriate choice when the preparation does not weaken cusps significantly.
- **One- or two-surface restorations in low-load areas**: Anterior teeth and premolars under moderate occlusal load are strong composite candidates.
- **Young patients with large pulps**: Aggressive tooth preparation for a crown risks pulpal exposure; composite preserves structure while pulp recedes with age.
- **Patient preference for tooth-colored, minimally invasive approach**: When functional and structural criteria are satisfied, patient-centered care supports conservative restoration.
- **Cusp tip preservation**: When all four main cusps remain intact and sound, composite can reinforce without the full-coverage sacrifice.

### Favor Crown (Indirect Restoration)

- **Existing large multi-surface restoration (≥3 surfaces or >50% intercuspal width)**: Residual tooth structure cannot adequately resist occlusal forces; composite fracture risk is elevated.
- **Cracked tooth or fractured cusp**: A crown splints the remaining tooth structure; composite cannot prevent crack propagation under cyclic load.
- **Endodontically treated posterior teeth**: Loss of proprioception and desiccation of dentin increases fracture susceptibility; crowns provide necessary cusp protection.
- **Heavily restored tooth with recurrent or secondary caries**: When secondary caries undermines an already large restoration, a crown may be the only means of achieving a durable, hygienic margin.
- **Bruxism or parafunctional habits**: High occlusal forces accelerate composite wear; a crown — especially full-cast metal or monolithic zirconia — provides superior wear resistance.
- **Abutment for fixed bridge**: Retention and resistance form requirements for bridge abutments typically exceed what direct restorations can provide.

## Contraindications

**Against composite when:**
- The tooth has insufficient sound enamel for bonding (poor prognosis for adhesive retention).
- Severe bruxism is uncontrolled and the patient refuses an occlusal guard.
- The preparation involves sub-gingival margins where moisture control is inadequate for bonding.

**Against crown when:**
- Tooth is adequately restored with composite and no structural or functional indication exists for upgrade.
- Periodontal or endodontic prognosis is uncertain; irreversible tooth preparation is premature.
- Patient cannot tolerate multi-appointment treatment or local anesthetic requirements.
- Cost and insurance constraints make a temporary composite a more pragmatic bridge to future care.

## Evidence Notes

- **Survival rates — direct composite**: A meta-analysis by Manhart et al. (2004, *Operative Dentistry*) reported annual failure rates of 1–3% for posterior composite restorations, with higher rates for larger restorations. 10-year survival for Class II composites in low-stress conditions approaches 80–90%.
- **Survival rates — crowns**: Pjetursson et al. (2018) reported 5-year survival of ~96% for all-ceramic crowns and ~95% for PFM crowns.
- **Cost-effectiveness**: Considering tooth preparation irreversibility and laboratory fees, composite restorations are more cost-effective for appropriately selected teeth. A 2020 study in *JADA* found that crown placement rates in the US vary 3-fold by region, suggesting clinical over-treatment in some markets.
- **Biomechanics**: The "isthmus width" rule (>1/3 IC width favors crown) is supported by finite element analysis studies (Panitvisai & Messer, 1995). However, modern adhesive composites with cuspal coverage techniques can extend the clinical range where direct restoration is viable.
- **Waterpik/margin integrity**: Crown margins at or below the gingival crest are associated with increased periodontal inflammation; composites with supragingival margins may offer a periodontal advantage in select cases.
