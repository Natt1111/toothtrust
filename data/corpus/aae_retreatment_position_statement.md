## Summary

Endodontic retreatment refers to the removal of existing root canal filling materials, re-instrumentation of the canal system, and re-obturation when a previously treated tooth fails to heal or develops new pathology. The AAE's position is that retreatment is a viable and often preferable alternative to extraction, particularly when tooth retention aligns with the patient's treatment goals and the tooth has a favorable prognosis.

The decision between retreatment, periradicular surgery (apicoectomy with retrograde fill), or extraction with implant placement is among the most consequential in dentistry and requires systematic evaluation of the existing treatment quality, presence of persistent infection, restorability, periodontal status, and patient factors including cost and systemic health.

---

## Clinical Indications

### Retreatment Is Indicated When:
- **Radiographic evidence of treatment failure**: Persistent or enlarging periapical radiolucency after initial root canal treatment (RCT), particularly when the lesion has not resolved within 12–24 months.
- **Inadequate initial obturation**: Underfilled canals (>2 mm short of apex), voids, or missed canals identified radiographically or by CBCT.
- **Signs or symptoms of persistent infection**: Sinus tract, recurrent swelling, pain on biting, or tenderness to percussion in a previously treated tooth.
- **Pre-prosthetic retreatment**: Prior to placing a new full-coverage restoration on a tooth with an uncertain or radiographically inadequate previous RCT, retreatment may be indicated to ensure long-term success.
- **Coronal leakage with contamination**: When the existing coronal restoration has failed and the canal system has been re-contaminated, retreatment before crown replacement is recommended.

### Retreatment vs. Periradicular Surgery
- **Retreatment preferred** when: inadequate obturation is the primary issue; the coronal anatomy allows re-access; existing restorative work does not mandate surgical approach.
- **Periradicular surgery preferred** when: a well-compacted, well-fitting post precludes non-surgical access; persistent apical pathology despite adequate orthograde retreatment; biopsy of a periapical lesion is needed; root anatomy (e.g., isthmus, fins) cannot be addressed orthogradely.
- **Combined approach**: Orthograde retreatment followed by surgical apicoectomy if retreatment alone fails; or simultaneous when indicated.

---

## Contraindications

- **Hopeless periodontal status**: Advanced bone loss, deep pockets, furcation involvement class III in molars — extraction and implant planning is more appropriate.
- **Non-restorable crown**: If the tooth cannot be restored to form and function after retreatment, the investment is not justified.
- **Severe root resorption**: Extensive internal or external resorption compromising root integrity.
- **Vertical root fracture (VRF)**: VRF is not salvageable with retreatment; hemisection or extraction is required.
- **Patient factors**: Inability to tolerate extended treatment, financial constraints, systemic conditions affecting healing (poorly controlled diabetes, bisphosphonate use with MRONJ risk).

## Evidence Notes

- **Success rates**: A meta-analysis by Torabinejad et al. (2009, *J Endod*) found radiographic healing rates of 77–83% for non-surgical retreatment of failed RCTs, compared to 85–93% for initial treatments — supporting retreatment as a viable option.
- **Retreatment vs. implant**: A systematic review (Iqbal & Kim, 2007) found no significant difference in 5-year survival between endodontically treated retained teeth and single implants, provided selection criteria were appropriate.
- **CBCT in retreatment planning**: Cone-beam CT significantly improves detection of missed canals, vertical root fractures, and complex anatomy compared to periapical radiographs (Patel et al., 2012). AAE recommends CBCT when periapical films are inconclusive.
- **Gutta-percha removal**: Ultrasonic files, rotary NiTi retreatment systems (ProTaper Retreatment, D-RaCe), and heat carriers are effective tools; no single method is universally superior for all anatomies.
- **Prognosis factors**: Teeth with preoperative periapical lesions and inadequate initial obturation have significantly higher retreatment success rates compared to those that had technically adequate treatment with persistent disease — suggesting biological rather than technical factors drive some failures.
