## Summary

The replacement of existing amalgam restorations with tooth-colored composite resin is a frequent patient request and a clinically nuanced decision. The ADA and FDA have both addressed amalgam safety; the 2023 FDA final rule recommends avoiding amalgam for vulnerable populations (children under 6, pregnant/nursing individuals, those with renal impairment), though it does not mandate removal of serviceable existing restorations in the general population.

Elective replacement driven solely by aesthetic preference must be balanced against the principle of "first, do no harm" — each replacement cycle removes additional tooth structure and risks pulpal damage. Replacement is clinically justified when existing restorations show clear signs of failure, secondary caries, fracture, or marginal breakdown.

---

## Clinical Indications

### Justified Replacement Criteria
- **Secondary (recurrent) caries**: Radiographic or clinical evidence of caries at or adjacent to existing amalgam margins is the primary indication for replacement.
- **Marginal breakdown and ditching**: Loss of marginal integrity allowing bacterial ingress; clinically detectable ditching >0.5 mm with radiographic evidence of marginal gap.
- **Fractured restoration**: Bulk fracture of the amalgam, particularly in high-load areas, requiring replacement to restore form and function.
- **Fractured tooth structure adjacent to restoration**: Cracked or fractured cusps associated with large amalgam restorations ("wedge effect").
- **Overhanging margins**: Amalgam overhangs that contribute to plaque retention and periodontal inflammation.
- **Exposed amalgam undermining tooth structure**: Loss of tooth walls previously supported by amalgam.
- **Patient in FDA-vulnerable category**: Pregnant patients, children under 6, or those with diagnosed renal impairment, where FDA 2023 guidance recommends amalgam avoidance.

### Elective/Aesthetic Replacement (Lower Priority)
- Serviceable amalgams in patients with documented metal hypersensitivity (rare; true amalgam allergy involves mercury or tin contact allergy confirmed by patch testing).
- Patient preference in anterior or visible areas where aesthetics are substantially compromised, provided the patient understands the additional tooth structure loss involved.

---

## Contraindications

- **Serviceable amalgam with no clinical or radiographic pathology**: Elective removal increases preparation size, risks pulpal insult, and contributes to the "restoration replacement cycle."
- **High caries risk patients without improved oral hygiene**: Composite at-risk margins may fail sooner than amalgam in high-caries environments.
- **Patients with active bruxism without occlusal guard**: Composite restorations are more susceptible to wear and fracture than amalgam under parafunctional load; replacement without addressing the parafunction is counterproductive.
- **Deep preparations near pulp**: Each replacement incrementally deepens the preparation; replacement in already-deep restorations increases pulpal exposure risk.

---

## Evidence Notes

- **Restoration replacement cycle**: Mjör & Toffenetti (2000) documented that replacement of existing restorations accounts for 50–75% of operative dentistry, and each replacement reduces remaining tooth structure by ~0.3–0.5 mm of depth. The "cycle" eventually necessitates endodontic treatment or crown.
- **Amalgam longevity**: Well-placed amalgam restorations can last 15–20+ years. A Cochrane review (Rasines Alcaraz et al., 2014) found no consistent superiority of composite over amalgam for posterior teeth in long-term clinical outcomes.
- **Mercury exposure**: The FDA's 2023 ruling was precautionary for vulnerable populations; blood mercury levels from well-placed amalgam are below EPA reference doses for most adults. The ADA maintains that existing serviceable amalgams should not be routinely removed.
- **Composite in high-load areas**: Posterior composites have higher polymerization shrinkage and wear compared to amalgam, particularly in Class II preparations in bruxers without occlusal guards.
- **Minamata Convention**: The global phase-down of dental amalgam under the Minamata Convention on Mercury is prompting material transitions internationally, influencing long-term material planning in dental practices.
