## Summary

A dental crown (full-coverage restoration) is indicated when a tooth has sustained sufficient structural damage, functional compromise, or aesthetic deficit that cannot be adequately addressed by a direct restoration such as composite resin or amalgam. The ADA and its Council on Scientific Affairs emphasize that crown placement should be driven by clinical necessity rather than material preference or reimbursement incentives. Proper diagnosis requires evaluation of remaining tooth structure, occlusal forces, pulpal status, periodontal health, and patient-specific risk factors.

Crowns serve as full coronal coverage restorations fabricated from metal alloys, porcelain-fused-to-metal (PFM), all-ceramic (zirconia, lithium disilicate), or combinations thereof. Selection among these materials depends on esthetic demands, occlusal load, and the extent of tooth preparation required.

## Clinical Indications

### Structural Indications
- **Fractured cusps or cracked tooth syndrome** where the fracture line extends beyond what direct bonding can stabilize; cusp fractures involving more than one-third of the occlusal table are generally considered crown candidates.
- **Large existing restorations** occupying more than 50% of the intercuspal distance or encompassing three or more surfaces, where the remaining tooth structure is insufficient to resist occlusal forces.
- **Severely worn dentition** due to bruxism, erosion, or attrition where vertical dimension of occlusion must be re-established.
- **Post-endodontic treatment** in posterior teeth: root canal treated molars and premolars lose structural integrity due to access cavity preparation and dentin desiccation; full-coverage crowns significantly reduce fracture risk.
- **Teeth with previous large amalgam restorations** showing marginal breakdown, secondary caries, or cusp fracture risk.

### Functional Indications
- Abutment teeth for fixed partial dentures (bridges) where the preparation is required for retainer placement.
- Teeth requiring alteration of axial contour or occlusal plane for prosthodontic purposes.
- Teeth that have undergone significant coronal destruction from caries where a buildup alone cannot provide adequate retention form for a direct restoration.

### Aesthetic Indications
- Severe intrinsic staining (tetracycline, fluorosis) unresponsive to bleaching.
- Significant morphological abnormalities (peg laterals, microdontia) where direct bonding cannot achieve adequate form.
- Full-mouth rehabilitation cases requiring uniform aesthetics and functional correction simultaneously.

### Pediatric Considerations
- Stainless steel crowns (SSC) for primary molars with multi-surface caries, pulpotomy-treated teeth, or hypoplastic teeth; SSCs outperform multi-surface composite restorations in longevity in primary dentition.

## Contraindications

- **Insufficient tooth structure for preparation**: teeth with minimal supragingival tooth structure may require surgical crown lengthening prior to crown preparation, or extraction may be more appropriate.
- **Uncontrolled periodontal disease**: crown margins placed on teeth with active periodontitis accelerate further attachment loss; periodontal stability must be achieved before definitive restorations.
- **Active caries not fully removed**: margins cannot be placed in carious dentin.
- **Immature apex in young permanent teeth**: where pulpal vitality is important for apexogenesis; direct restoration or pulp therapy is preferred until root maturation is complete.
- **Questionable restorability**: if the prognosis is hopeless due to root resorption, inadequate bone support, or non-restorable fracture, extraction and implant planning may be more appropriate.
- **Patient non-compliance**: patients who refuse or cannot maintain adequate oral hygiene are at elevated risk for secondary caries at crown margins.

## Evidence Notes

The ADA has historically supported evidence-based criteria for crown placement. Key literature findings include:

- **Longevity data**: All-ceramic crowns (lithium disilicate, zirconia) demonstrate 5-year survival rates of 93–96% in posterior regions (Pjetursson et al., 2018, *Journal of Clinical Periodontology*).
- **Post-endo crowns**: Iqbal & Kim (2007, *Journal of Endodontics*) found a 6× higher survival rate for endodontically treated posterior teeth restored with crowns vs. direct restorations at 5 years.
- **Decision thresholds**: The "50% rule" for existing restoration size is supported by biomechanical studies showing increased cusp fracture risk as the isthmus width exceeds half the intercuspal distance (Reeh et al., 1992).
- **Overtreatment concern**: A 2019 ADA Health Policy Institute report flagged geographic variation in crown rates inconsistent with population-level caries prevalence, underscoring the need for clinical necessity documentation.
- **Material selection**: Zirconia monolithic crowns have demonstrated reduced chipping vs. PFM in randomized controlled trials with 3-year follow-up (Heintze & Rousson, 2010).
